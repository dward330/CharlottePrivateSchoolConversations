Middle School Clubs (Grades 6–8)
Middle School runs on a rotating sampler model: students choose from more than 25 offerings, meet once a
week for an 8–10 week cycle in 30-minute sessions, then rotate to a different club. The design is deliberately
exposure-oriented — the goal is breadth rather than a single-season commitment. Volunteers and faculty advise
the sessions. [Source: charlottelatin.org — Clubs]
The School names five of the more popular Middle School offerings: Cooking Club, Just Dance Club, Robotics
Club, Student Council, and Sports Club. Two of these touch the arts/sports boundary (Just Dance leans
toward dance; Sports Club toward athletics); they are reported here because the School lists them as general
Middle School clubs, with the boundary noted. In addition, the Middle School fields competitive academic teams
that are in clear scope — Science Olympiad (Division B) and a Middle School Speech & Debate offering for
7th and 8th graders. [Sources: charlottelatin.org; NC Science Olympiad]

Why These Three Are Combined
This dossier merges three operational questions — how clubs are governed and formed, how they are funded,
and how students discover them. They are combined because Charlotte Latin publishes solid information on
governance and formation but relatively little on club budgets and on a formal discovery event; rather than ship
two thin documents, the funding and discovery material is folded in here with its gaps clearly marked.
Governance & Advising
Charlotte Latin clubs operate under faculty and volunteer guidance. In the Middle School, volunteers and faculty
advise the weekly 30-minute club sessions. In the Upper School, clubs enrich student life across service, global
studies, and leadership, and the School — corroborated by third-party reviews — is described as supportive of
student-led clubs. The consistent structural requirement across divisions is that a club has a faculty advisor.
[Sources: charlottelatin.org — Clubs; Niche]
Two governance bodies sit above the individual clubs. The elected Student Council plans events and channels
student concerns to administration, and the Honor Council (supported by its Advisory Board) administers the
Honor Code. Together they give the club ecosystem an elected student-government layer. [Source:
charlottelatin.org — Clubs]

Structural Participation (the Strongest Evidence)
The clearest participation signal is structural rather than numerical. In the Middle School, club time is built into
the weekly schedule and every student selects a club each 8–10 week cycle, so Middle School club participation
is effectively near-universal by design — students rotate through several clubs across a year. In the Upper
School, clubs meet during designated periods in the school day, lowering the barrier to joining relative to schools
where everything is after-school. [Source: charlottelatin.org — Clubs; Return-to-Campus schedule notes]

How Clubs Cluster Into Pathways
Individually, a club is a weekly meeting. Grouped, clubs form pathways — sets of activities that, taken together,
build toward a recognizable interest area or post-secondary direction. This dossier maps Charlotte Latin's
non-sport, non-arts clubs into five pathways and explains the mechanism: what a cluster exposes students to and
how. The framing is descriptive of exposure and demonstrated interest, not a promise of any outcome (e.g.,
admission or a career).
Pathway 1 — Civic, Political & Public-Voice
This is Charlotte Latin's deepest non-arts pathway. Speech & Debate (a top-20 national program) and its feeder
Forensics Club build argument, evidence, and public-speaking skill; Model United Nations applies those skills
to diplomacy and policy; World Quest tests current-affairs and geopolitical knowledge against other schools.
Governance experience comes from the elected Student Council and the Honor Council Advisory Board. A
student who strings these together gets sustained exposure to exactly the skills that surface in law, government,
journalism, and policy — reasoning under pressure, structured argument, and public deliberation. [Sources:
charlottelatin.org; NSDA]

international trips. Language and culture clubs — Spanish Club, Table Française, and the National Junior

Pathway 3 — STEM & Innovation
The STEM cluster is anchored by the Fab Lab, an official outgrowth of MIT's Center for Bits and Atoms,

computational thinking. A well-known proof point: a student-led Fab Lab campaign produced 3D-printed and

Entrepreneurship is a real but narrower thread, carried more by coursework and the Fab Lab than by a dedicated

multidisciplinary approach to creative problem-solving, and the Fab Lab gives students a place to prototype

Honest limit: this pathway leans on a course plus the Fab Lab rather than a club. If a business or entrepreneurship club exists
term-to-term, it is not in the School's published roster.
Pathway 5 — Service & Leadership (Cross-Reference)
Charlotte Latin's largest cluster by sheer club count is service and leadership — the Service Council slate,
Blessings in a Backpack, Club Sandwich, Paws for the Cause, CLS Math Tutors, Girl Up, SADD, Admissions
Ambassadors, and the leadership bodies. Because it is so substantial, it is treated in its own dossier (Service,
Leadership & Civic Engagement) rather than compressed here.
Reading the Pathways Honestly
Two cautions. First, these clusters describe exposure: joining Model UN exposes a student to diplomacy and
research; it does not 'get them noticed' for anything by itself. Second, the strongest, most externally validated
pathways at Charlotte Latin are the civic/public-voice and STEM clusters, because they have national results and
dedicated facilities behind them. The global, entrepreneurship, and (within this dossier) service threads are real
but rest more on program structure than on competitive hardware.
