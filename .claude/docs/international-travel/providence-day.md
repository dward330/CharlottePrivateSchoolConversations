Japan
Continuity and Change
Registration Coming Soon

Open To: Current 9-11 grade students

Program Highlights
Japan invites students into a country where the past is never quite past, where ancient ritual and modern invention share the same city block, and where a society has learned to carry difficult history alongside extraordinary creativity. Moving from the energy of Tokyo to the quieter streets of Hiroshima and the sacred island of Miyajima, this program asks students to explore how a nation remembers, adapts, and reimagines itself across generations.
Throughout the program, students engage directly with Japanese daily life, from the rhythm of the world's most efficient transit system to the markets where food tells a story of place and the neighborhoods where youth culture and centuries-old tradition sit side by side. Through hands-on workshops, immersive museums, and time in the districts where Japan's creative industries took shape, they consider how the country absorbs outside influence and transforms it into something distinctly its own, and how creativity, craftsmanship, and collective memory shape the Japan students encounter today.
The journey also makes space for one of the twentieth century's most important stories. In Hiroshima, students stand in a city that experienced the unimaginable and chose to become a global voice for peace, rebuilding not only its buildings but its rivers, parks, and tree-lined streets. Rather than treating this history in isolation, the program holds it alongside Japan's living culture, allowing students to understand remembrance not as an ending but as one of the ways a society shapes its future. By the program's conclusion, students return home with a fuller picture of Japan and a clearer sense of their own roles as thoughtful, empathetic global citizens.

Day 1
The program officially begins upon our arrival in Tokyo on our overnight international flight. We take the train into the city, experiencing Japan's remarkably efficient transit system for the first time. After checking into our hotel and settling in, we review expectations, local customs, and health and safety protocols.
In the evening, we share our first meal at a local izakaya, easing into Japanese daily life over good food and conversation. A short walk back to the hotel offers an initial sense of the city's rhythm before an early night after a long day of travel.
Day 2
Our first full day in Tokyo begins with a visit to Yokoamicho Park. Here, an activity sets memorials and artifacts from the 1945 firebombings against the contemporary cityscape that surrounds them, inviting reflection on how a city carries its history while continuing to build everyday life around it.
Following lunch, the day shifts as a walking tour takes us through Meiji Jingu, a serene forested shrine dedicated to the Emperor Meiji, whose wooded grounds feel worlds away from the streets just beyond them. Afterward, the short walk into neighboring Harajuku could not offer a sharper contrast, as we step into one of Tokyo's most exuberant centers of youth culture, fashion, and self-expression, with time to explore the shops and side streets that make the neighborhood famous. Moving between these two spaces in a single afternoon sets up a question the program returns to again and again: how tradition and reinvention live side by side here.
Day 3
We set out for teamLab Planets, where immersive, walk-through digital artworks dissolve the line between viewer and artwork and offer a striking introduction to Japan's contemporary creative scene.
From there, we make our way to the Tsukiji fish market, taking in the energy and abundance of one of Tokyo's most famous food destinations, with an early lunch among its stalls.
In the afternoon, we head to Akihabara, the district synonymous with electronics, anime, and gaming culture. Here, students take part in a manga drawing workshop, gaining hands-on experience with one of Japan's most globally influential art forms and considering how stories communicate values, identity, and social commentary. Afterward, time to explore the surrounding neighborhood lets us see firsthand how Japan's creative industries shape and are shaped by the wider world.
Day 4
We board the Shinkansen, Japan’s high-speed bullet train, and settle in for the roughly five-hour journey to Hiroshima, enjoying bento box lunches as the landscape of Honshu passes outside our windows at speeds approaching two hundred miles per hour. The ride is a genuine experience in its own right, and it also offers time to rest, journal, and reflect on our days in Tokyo.
Upon arrival, we make our way to the hotel before setting out for Hiroshima Castle. We explore the grounds and learn about the region's feudal history, as well as the castle's destruction in the atomic bombing and its postwar reconstruction. Encountering this layered history on our first afternoon establishes a foundation for the days ahead, reminding us that Hiroshima's story extends far beyond a single moment in 1945.
Day 5
Today we start with a Japanese sweets-making class. Under the guidance of local instructors, we try our hand at wagashi, the delicate traditional confections served with tea and shaped to reflect the seasons.
Following lunch, we visit the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park for a guided tour through the Atomic Bomb Dome, the Peace Memorial Museum, and the surrounding grounds. Moving through these spaces, along with a possible survivor testimony, offers a deeply human perspective on the atomic bombing and its aftermath, and we confront both the immediate devastation of that day and the city's remarkable commitment to peace in the decades since.
The park itself tells part of that story. Built on ground that was leveled in an instant, it is now green and deliberately planted, and across the city stand the survivor trees that endured the blast and continue to grow today. Predictions at the time held that nothing would grow here for decades, and Hiroshima's recovery of its rivers, parks, and tree-lined streets became its own quiet argument about resilience. Afterward, we take time to debrief our experience both individually and as a group.
Day 6
We travel by public transit and ferry to Miyajima Island, long considered one of the most sacred sites in Japan. The morning is spent exploring Itsukushima Shrine with its iconic floating torii gate, the hillside temple complex of Daisho-in, and the lively Omotesando shopping street, where students experience a different rhythm of Japanese life.
After lunch on the island, we visit Senjokaku Pavilion and the adjacent Five-Story Pagoda before ascending Mt. Misen by ropeway. Near the summit stands the site of Reikado Hall, which for centuries sheltered a sacred flame said to have burned continuously for over a thousand years, the same fire used to light the Flame of Peace in Hiroshima's Peace Memorial Park. The hall was lost to fire in May of 2026, but the flame itself was carried to safety and preserved, and the site is being rebuilt. That story of loss, rescue, and renewal gives us a fitting place to pause: with views stretching over the Seto Inland Sea, the group takes time to reflect, journal, and share how their understanding of remembrance has evolved since arriving in Hiroshima.
Day 7
On the Shinkansen train back to Tokyo, we enjoy bento box lunches and watch the landscape we crossed days earlier unspool in reverse, this time carrying everything we have seen and learned along the way.
After dropping our luggage at the hotel, we make our way to the Center of the Tokyo Air Raids, where students take part in a simulation set in 1951 during the signing of the Treaty of San Francisco. Acting as members of a joint historical commission, we consider how nations shape public memory in order to support political stability and alliance building. Afterward, we take time to personally reflect on the experience.
Day 8
Our final full day in Japan begins with breakfast and a briefing before we visit the Tokyo National Museum, where collections spanning ancient artifacts to classical art trace the long arc of Japanese artistic tradition. Encountering these works near the end of the journey gives shape to much of what we have seen over the past week, from the temples of Miyajima to the craft traditions of Hiroshima.
After lunch, we set out to explore the city, returning to favorite neighborhoods and seeking out corners we may have missed earlier in the week. It is a chance to gather gifts and mementos and to put to use the confidence and street sense the group has built along the way.
We share a final celebratory dinner together before returning to the hotel for our evening debrief and time to pack before bed.
Day 9
Our final morning begins with breakfast, followed by a closing ceremony in which students mark the end of the program, giving everyone space to reflect on what they have experienced and how they have grown.
As we board our flight home, we carry with us the contrasts of this country, the energy of Tokyo, the beauty of Miyajima, and the quiet resolve of Hiroshima, along with a deeper understanding of how a society can hold all of those things at once.

Lenses of Inquiry
ADOPT, ADAPT, CREATE
Japan's global cultural influence is tied to a remarkable ability to take in ideas, refine them through craftsmanship and precision, and make them into something unmistakably its own. From the classical collections of the Tokyo National Museum to the manga studios and electronics districts of the contemporary city, and from the youth culture of Harajuku to the traditional practices of Hiroshima and the artistry of Miyajima, students encounter a country where creative expression runs through every register of daily life. Through hands-on workshops, immersive digital art, neighborhood explorations, and time in places where the historical and the modern sit side by side, students consider how creative practice functions as a kind of cultural language, how borrowed ideas get localized and transformed rather than simply imported, and how a country can change across centuries without losing its sense of self.
TRADITION, RITUAL, AND EVERYDAY LIFE
Japan is a country where sacred space and city life coexist in close quarters, where a forested shrine can sit a few minutes from a crowded shopping street, and where the rituals of food, craft, and community remain woven into daily routines. Through visits to shrines and temples, time in markets and neighborhoods, and encounters with the small courtesies that structure Japanese social life, students explore how tradition is sustained not only in monuments but in ordinary practice. This lens invites reflection on how a highly modern society preserves and adapts its customs, and on what students notice about belonging, hospitality, and rhythm when daily life looks different from their own.
HISTORICAL REMEMBRANCE AND THE WORK OF PEACE
In Hiroshima, students encounter a city that experienced one of history's gravest moments and responded by dedicating itself to peace. Through the Peace Memorial Park and Museum, conversation with someone who carries the memory of 1945, and time at the Atomic Bomb Dome, students engage with testimonies and artifacts that make plain both the human cost of conflict and the resilience of a community in its aftermath. They also consider the recovery of the city itself, from the trees that survived the blast and still stand today to the parks and waterways that shape modern Hiroshima, evidence of how a place rebuilds its living environment as well as its buildings.
The visit to Hiroshima Castle adds another layer, connecting the region's feudal past to its modern identity and showing how a single place can hold more than one history at once. This lens guides students to consider how remembrance can serve as a catalyst for peace-building, and to reflect on their own roles in fostering empathy, dialogue, and understanding in a complicated world.

Dietary and Allergy Considerations when Visiting Japan
Japanese cuisine is built around ingredients such as gluten, soy, MSG, and fish broth, which are present in a large share of dishes. Culturally, meals are served as the chef designed them, so restaurants usually do not prepare special food on request. When they do, the options are simple and limited in variety, such as meat, fish, or vegetables boiled or steamed without seasoning. Kitchens do not tend to keep utensils, surfaces, or tools separate from allergens, so cross-contamination cannot be ruled out.
Our tour leaders, in partnership with our travel partner Envoys, assume a duty of care for all students on our programs. Managing dietary restrictions in Japan relies on the school, the families, and our tour partners planning the program together, and depending on the severity and the number of cases within a group, different measures may be proposed. These may include adding a Field Staff Member dedicated to sourcing safe meals, faculty support with meal logistics on the ground, families bringing supplementary food from home, or dividing the group for certain meals. Some of these measures may carry an additional cost.
However, all travelers are subject to the food culture and practices of the country we are traveling to, and special meal requests cannot be guaranteed in Japan. Parents are advised to review these considerations during the program decision-making process.

This journey through Iceland immerses students in one of the world’s most environmentally forward-thinking nations. Throughout the program, students explore the intersections of climate science, renewable innovation, and global sustainability. As they journey across glaciers, geothermal fields, and coastal ecosystems, students witness firsthand the effects of climate change in one of the world’s most dynamic environments.
Visits to Iceland's geothermal power infrastructure and a working greenhouse help students examine how the country leads in renewable energy and innovation, while community engagement along the south coast and in Reykjavík gives students direct, ground-level insight into how Icelanders are putting sustainability into practice in their own lives.
Throughout the program, the group engages with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals as a framework for inquiry, using Iceland as a case study to assess the relationship between local practices and global priorities.

Day 1
After an overnight flight to Reykjavík, we explore Reykjavík, enjoying lunch along the way. In the afternoon, we visit the Perlan Museum to experience the Wonders of Iceland exhibit.
Day 2
Today is dedicated to one of Iceland’s most iconic routes, the Golden Circle. We depart Reykjavík on our private bus and begin with a visit to Þingvellir National Park, where we learn about its geological significance and role in Icelandic history. We then continue to Friðheimar Greenhouse for lunch and a guided tour of the tomato greenhouse, gaining insight into sustainable agriculture in a unique environment. Afterward, we visit the Geysir geothermal area and the powerful Gullfoss waterfall.
Our afternoon continues in Laugarvatn with a geothermal bread making experience, where we learn how residents have long used the earth's heat to bake bread underground, a simple and memorable window into how deeply geothermal energy shapes daily life. We then complete our route with a stop at Kerið crater before returning to Reykjavík.
Day 3
Our focus shifts south as the group travels along Iceland’s scenic coastline. We depart by private bus for the Lava Center in Hvolsvöllur, where interactive exhibits and simulations bring the science of Iceland's volcanic activity to life.
Following lunch in the area, we continue to Skógafoss to explore both Skógafoss and Kvernufoss waterfalls, taking in the scale and force of Iceland's flowing water up close. The group then visits Dyrhólaey to take in sweeping views of the arch and surrounding cliffs.
By evening, we arrive in Kirkjubæjarklaustur, where we check into our hotel.
Day 4
We depart east toward Iceland’s glacial landscapes. Our morning includes visits to Jökulsárlón Glacier Lagoon and nearby Diamond Beach, where icebergs create a striking contrast against the dark sand. After lunch in the area, we transfer to our guided glacier hike, giving us a hands-on opportunity to learn about glacial movement, climate, and terrain. After several hours on the ice, we return to Kirkjubæjarklaustur.
Day 5
We depart for Raufarhólshellir, where we explore the lava tunnel and walk through underground formations created by volcanic activity. Lunch follows in Hveragerði before we continue to the Hellisheiði Power Station in Ölfus, where we learn how Iceland harnesses natural geothermal energy to power homes and communities across the country. In the late afternoon, we return to Reykjavík.
Day 6
Our final full day in Iceland focuses on community engagement and reflection, meeting with local Icelanders working on sustainability initiatives, giving students the chance to ask questions and hear firsthand how residents are putting the SDGs into practice in their own communities.
Following lunch, we transfer to Sky Lagoon, where we get the opportunity to relax in the geothermal waters after a physically active week. The afternoon includes a closing ceremony, creating space to reflect on learning and shared experiences. Our day concludes with a farewell dinner.
Day 7
If time permits, we explore Reykjavík one last time, visiting Hallgrimskirkja Church or picking up final gifts for our loved ones back home, before heading to the airport.

Lenses of Inquiry
ICELAND'S GLACIERS AND A WARMING WORLD
Iceland’s glaciers are some of the clearest and most dramatic indicators of global climate change. Once seemingly permanent, these vast ice formations are now retreating at alarming rates, reshaping ecosystems, raising sea levels, and disrupting communities and industries that depend on them. As students hike across glacial terrain and observe meltwater lagoons, they experience the fragility of these environments firsthand.
Throughout the program, they explore the scientific, social, economic, and ethical dimensions of glacial retreat, deepening their understanding of the far-reaching impacts of a warming world.
ENERGY AND INNOVATION
Iceland is a global leader in renewable energy, generating nearly all its electricity and heat from geothermal and hydropower. In a world increasingly constrained by the limits of fossil fuels, Iceland offers a compelling case study in aligning natural resources with sustainable innovation. Through visits to local sites, students explore how Iceland harnesses Earth’s internal heat to power communities, cut emissions, and sustain food production in subarctic conditions.
LIVING THE SDGS
The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) provide a blueprint for a more just and sustainable world. Iceland, though small in population, plays an outsized role in advancing these goals through environmental policies, clean energy innovation, and natural heritage preservation. This lens frames the program as a living laboratory for the SDGs, inviting students to assess how local practices align with global goals. As they engage with Iceland’s landscapes, technologies, and communities, students identify success stories and ongoing challenges. Through structured reflection and collaborative inquiry, they explore how interconnected issues, like climate action (SDG 13), responsible consumption (SDG 12), and life below water (SDG 14), unfold in real-world settings.

Paris:
This program in Paris invites students to explore the city as a living classroom where history, art, and daily life intersect. Moving through iconic landmarks such as Notre Dame, the Eiffel Tower, and the Musée d'Orsay, students examine how Paris has shaped and been shaped by centuries of political power, cultural production, and social change. ## 11. Gaps & questions to ask the school

1. **Starting grade:** What is the earliest grade allowed on an international trip? Are
 Middle School trips open to 6th graders, or only to 7th–8th graders?
2. **Current trip list:** Which trips are offered for 2026–27 and summer 2027? What are the
 dates, durations and grade bands?
3. **Cost:** What is the price range per trip, and what does it include (airfare,
 insurance, meals)? What are the deposit and refund policies?
4. **Aid:** Do the Murdock, Latchwood or Bhandari Mehta "beyond tuition" funds cover
 global trips? How does a family apply, and when?
5. **Jordan/IAA:** How are students selected for the Daher-funded trip? Which grades are
 eligible, when does it happen, and is it fully funded?
6. **Selection:** Are trips first-come, first-served or application-based? How are Round
 Square delegates chosen?
7. **Safety:** What are the chaperone ratios, travel vendor(s), insurance, emergency
 protocols, and passport and visa timelines?
8. **Exchanges:** How do families apply for Round Square exchanges? Are host families
 required to host in return?
9. **GSD:** Is the points system still used? Does one trip satisfy the travel
 requirement? Is there a GSD-specific travel subsidy?
10. **Recognition:** What was the July 2026 "national recognition," and which organization
 gave it?
11. **Scale:** How many students travel abroad each year, and what share of each grade
 does that represent?

`$1000`). The app authors these as `$5,650`, `$5,450`, `$3,850`, `$2,700`, `$1,600`,

amounts are unchanged. Dates are written with an en dash (`June 4–13, 2027`) where the
pages use a hyphen.

existing "Lycée Notre Dame de la Merci" so one school reads identically across the ledger
