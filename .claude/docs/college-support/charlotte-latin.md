SEGMENT 4 — APPLICATION SUPPORT
The Application Itself
A three-person essay team, review before submission, and the two money conversations kept
carefully separate.
This segment covers the machinery of the application itself — essays, letters, review, and the two distinct
money conversations (college-awarded merit versus the school's own tuition aid). Charlotte Latin's most
distinctive application-support asset is a dedicated three-person essay team layered on top of the
counselors.
Essay support: a dedicated three-person team
Three named essay specialists support seniors — Andrea Powell, Marty Minchin, and Mary Beth Foster ’99.
Minchin is a professional writer and editor who teaches college-level writing at UNC Charlotte; Foster is a
Charlotte-area writer and a Latin alumna. Support runs from optional summer essay workshops the summer
before senior year, through iterative essay review and feedback, into a senior “College Application
Bootcamp.” Having writing specialists distinct from the college counselors is an unusual amount of
dedicated essay capacity for a school this size.
Letters, and review before submission
(cid:127) Counselor letters. Counselors write the school's letter of recommendation and advocate individually for
each student.
(cid:127) Every application reviewed. The senior-year process explicitly includes proofreading each essay and
application prior to submission — so a second set of eyes sees an application before it goes out.
Early Decision / Early Action
ED/EA strategy is handled through senior seminars on the application process and junior-year
testing-and-timeline discussions, within the school's adherence to the NACAC principles of good practice.
The school does not publish specific ED/EA policy language; families should confirm the office's current
guidance directly.
The two money conversations — kept separate
College-awarded merit scholarships
Charlotte Latin students have historically won marquee college merit awards, including the Morehead-Cain
and Robertson Scholarships at UNC-Chapel Hill — a 2022 senior received the Morehead-Cain, which
admits a low single-digit percentage of applicants and covers the full cost of attendance.
College-merit dollar totals are not published
The school does not publish aggregate college-merit-scholarship dollars or breadth for recent classes. Named
marquee awards are documented; a class-wide total is not. This is a transparency gap on the college-merit
question specifically — ask the office for recent totals and for mid-tier merit at schools like Alabama, South
Carolina, or Miami.

People, Timing & Tools
Four counselors, a freshman-year start, and SCOIR-powered admissions data.
Charlotte Latin's College Counseling Office is a four-counselor operation with a long-stable core, a
dedicated essay-support team, and a counselor assigned to every student from the freshman year. This
segment lays out who staffs the office, when individualized college work begins, and the concrete data tools
families can use.
Who staffs the office
The 2025–26 Upper School Profile lists the office as four full-time counselors, a registrar, an administrative
assistant, and three essay specialists. The counseling roles are held by:

Counselor-to-student load. With a 2025–26 senior class of about 145 and Upper School enrollment of
roughly 586 across grades 9–12, four counselors work out to approximately 36–37 seniors per counselor at
the point of most intensive application work, and roughly 145 Upper School students per counselor when
spread across all four grades. The three essay specialists sit on top of that ratio as added senior-year
capacity.
When individualized work begins
Every student is assigned a college counselor in the freshman year — language the school has used
consistently since at least its 2017–18 profile — so the counselor relationship is built over four years rather
than compressed into junior and senior year. Grade 9 programming opens with college-counseling seminars
and student registration in SCOIR, the office's data platform; grade-appropriate seminars then run every
year through senior spring (self-discovery and interest-mapping in the underclass years; testing timelines,
essays, interviews, and the application mechanics later).
Staff stability
The office grew from three counselors in 2017–18 to four by roughly 2019, and the four-counselor core —
Jennings, Cripps, Smith, and Segura — appears consistently across the 2022–23, 2023–24, and 2025–26
profiles. That multi-year continuity is a genuine strength for families, since the same counselor is likely to
carry a student from assignment through graduation. Support-role titles (registrar and administrative
assistant) have seen some turnover across editions.

The data tools families get
Charlotte Latin uses SCOIR (pronounced “score”) as its college-planning and predictive-data platform;
college information is made available to families through MyLatin and SCOIR, and freshmen complete
SCOIR registration as part of grade-9 programming.
SCOIR's centerpiece for families is the scattergram: for a given college, it plots a student's GPA against
SAT/ACT results (or a concordance-converted PSAT/NMSQT score when a student has no SAT/ACT yet)
over the school's own history of admits, denials, and waitlists at that college — a data-grounded way to
gauge where a student stands. The high school controls how scattergrams appear: which GPA type is
shown, the range of graduating classes included, and a minimum number of prior acceptances (SCOIR's
default is five) before any data is displayed, and it can turn predictive display on or off.
What isn't published
The school's specific SCOIR configuration — exactly which scattergram settings and predictive features families
can see, and the class-year range shown — is not published publicly. Families can confirm it directly with the
office; its absence from public materials is a transparency limitation, not a sign the tool is unavailable.

SEGMENT 7 — FIT, RANK & THE WHOLE CLASS
The Honest Questions
No class rank, an explicit fit-over-prestige stance, learning-differences support, and room to
change direction.
The honest questions are about fit and the whole class: how the school keeps high achievers from crowding
one another out, how it supports students who aren't at the top, how it serves students with learning
differences, and whether it defaults to prestige or genuinely optimizes for fit. Charlotte Latin's published
stance leans clearly toward fit and the full class.
Class rank: not reported
Charlotte Latin does not report class rank — a policy stated consistently from the 2017–18 profile through
the current 2025–26 edition. Not ranking is the standard independent-school approach to the crowding
problem: it prevents a school's own strong students from being pitted against one another in a single ordinal
list, so two excellent students aren't separated by a hundredth of a GPA point in a college's eyes.
Fit over prestige, and support beyond the top
The office frames its work around fit rather than a prestige ladder. The Upper School describes the program
as “proactive and student-centered, helping each student identify schools that align with their goals and
passions,” and states plainly: “Urban or pastoral, in state or out of state, Division I or Division III — what
matters most are the dreams students carry, and the colleges that will help them achieve these dreams.”
The breadth of the acceptance list — which spans from open-access and regional universities to the most
selective institutions — is consistent with a whole-class model rather than a top-decile focus.
Students with learning differences
Two offices support students who learn differently. The Learning Resources office (established 1985)
provides teaching, screening, and consultation for documented learning differences, and the newer Office of
Academic Transition and Student Success (ATSS) supports executive functioning, self-advocacy, and
transitions. The college process intersects with these supports.
Accommodation-specific college counseling isn't detailed publicly
While the Learning Resources and ATSS offices are documented, the published materials do not detail
accommodation-specific college-process counseling — for example, disability-disclosure strategy or securing
extended-time documentation for college entrance testing. Families for whom this matters should ask how the
two offices and the college counselors coordinate on it.
When a student changes direction
The fit-first framing and the range of the acceptance list indicate real support for non-traditional directions:
dedicated arts schools (SCAD, Ringling, the School of the Art Institute of Chicago, the School of the
Museum of Fine Arts), international universities (McGill, St Andrews, University College London, Toronto,
Amsterdam, Leiden, Queensland), and the service academies all appear among recent destinations.

SEGMENT 3 — INSTITUTIONAL LEVERAGE
How the School's Name Works for Your Child
The School Profile as a leverage document, a nationally capped Malone designation and a Cum
Laude chapter, and the on-campus channels to colleges.
A School Profile is the document colleges receive alongside every transcript, and Charlotte Latin's is built to
make its students legible to an admissions reader. This segment covers how the profile does that work, the
two credentials the school leads with, and the on-campus channels that connect students to colleges
directly.
How the School Profile contextualizes a student
The profile is written to help an admissions officer interpret a Charlotte Latin transcript correctly. It lays out
the semester system and 21-credit graduation requirements, the benchmark gates for Honors/AP
placement, and — pointedly — the note that the Honors math track is so fast-paced that “many fine math
students are placed in ‘standard’ courses.” That single sentence tells a reader not to penalize a
standard-track placement, which is precisely the leverage a good profile provides: it reframes the transcript
in the school's favor without inflating anything.
The two credentials the school leads with
North Carolina's only Malone Scholars School
Charlotte Latin is the only Malone Scholars School in North Carolina. The Malone Family Foundation caps
the program at 50 independent schools nationally and selects for academic caliber, staff quality,
accommodations for gifted and talented students, strong AP/IB and enrichment programs, financial strength,
a commitment to financial aid, and a diverse population. Being one of a nationally capped group is a
concrete, verifiable marker an admissions reader can weigh.
Cum Laude Society chapter
The school holds a Cum Laude Society chapter — the Edward J. Fox Chapter, chartered in 1976 when the
school was six years old — which signals scholastic honor in the same way a college Phi Beta Kappa
chapter does. The school describes itself as the youngest ever to receive a chapter; that specific claim
appears in the school's own materials and is repeated here as the school's statement rather than as
independently verified fact.
Getting students in front of colleges
(cid:127) Rep Visits. The school runs a structured college-representative visit program on campus; families and
reps arrange visits through the registrar, and visits are logged so students can meet admissions officers
directly.
(cid:127) Dean's Panel. The senior year includes a college-admissions panel event.
(cid:127) Case Studies Mock College Admission + College Fair. Sophomores work through a hands-on
simulation of an admissions committee, paired with a College Fair — demystifying how decisions actually
get made.
(cid:127) Interview practice is addressed through seminars on effective interview strategy.
