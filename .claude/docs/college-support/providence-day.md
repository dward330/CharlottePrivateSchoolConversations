Staffing and counselor-to-student ratio
Providence Day's College Guidance office is led by a Director of College Guidance and, as of the 2025–26
school year, four Associate Directors of College Guidance, supported by an administrative assistant. The
school's own program page describes the model plainly: “Each college counselor works with one fourth of
each Upper School class, allowing us to place individual attention at the core of our program.” With 176–178
seniors per class in recent years, that quarter-class model works out to roughly 44–45 seniors per counselor
— a caseload far below typical public-school ratios, which often run in the hundreds.
Note a small documentation lag worth knowing: the marketing-facing College Guidance page still says “staffed
by four experienced college counselors,” while the current staff listing shows a Director plus four Associate
Directors (five credentialed counselors). The office has grown, and the published ratio language has not
caught up.
Counselor backgrounds and stability
(cid:127) Director tenure: the current Director of College Guidance has led the office since at least the 2017–18
School Profile and stated an 18-year tenure at Providence Day as of fall 2024 — nearly two decades of
continuity in the same seat.
(cid:127) Director credentials: a B.A. from Amherst College and an M.A. from the Bread Loaf School of English at
Middlebury College, plus leadership service (development professional and co-chair) with the Southern
Association for College Admission Counseling (SACAC).
(cid:127) Former admissions officers on staff — yes: one current Associate Director came to Providence Day
from the undergraduate admissions office of the University of California, Berkeley, and earlier worked at the
college-access nonprofit Collegiate Directions, Inc. This directly answers the “any former admissions
officers?” question in the affirmative.
(cid:127) Team continuity: one Associate Director has served since at least 2017–18. The office composition has
evolved (three counselors in 2018–19 to five today) through additions rather than turnover at the top.
(cid:127) Professional footprint: the team visits over 50 college campuses per year, attends and presents at
professional conferences, and serves on regional and national admissions boards. The school holds
NACAC, SACAC, and ACCIS memberships and complies with NACAC's Guide to Ethical Practice in

Class rank — and how high achievers avoid crowding each other out
(cid:127) Providence Day does not rank students — stated flatly in the School Profile.
(cid:127) The replacement mechanism is the quintile table: the Profile shows the weighted-GPA distribution of the
senior class by fifths, each with mean SAT and ACT. A reader can see that the Class of 2025's top fifth
averaged a 4.474 weighted GPA / 1479 SAT / 33 ACT — so a student at 4.3 is visibly strong without being
numbered against classmates.
(cid:127) Stated AP-load norms (2/3/4 by grade) protect students from an arms race the school itself would have to
disclose; “most demanding” has a defined, published meaning.
(cid:127) The practical effect: multiple students can present as top-of-class candidates to different colleges without a
rank forcing a single #1.
Support for students not at the top of the class
(cid:127) The published outcome floor is universal: 100% of graduates attend four-year colleges. The acceptance
list's breadth is the middle of the class made visible — alongside the most selective names sit East
Carolina, Appalachian State, High Point, Coastal Carolina, Wingate, Belmont, and dozens of regional
options, plus community colleges (Central Piedmont appears, bolded).
(cid:127) The quarter-class counselor model is not reserved for stars; every senior gets the same assigned
counselor and the same individual fall meetings.
(cid:127) The office's stated philosophy centers the whole class: “Our primary goal is to help students find the college
that is the best academic, extracurricular, social, and financial fit,” with explicit encouragement toward in-
and out-of-state, public and private options.
Learning differences through the college process
(cid:127) Providence Day staffs Learning Specialists at every division, including two dedicated to the Upper
School, whose published role is helping students “become successful learners and self-advocates” and
liaising among teachers, counselors, and outside educational specialists — the self-advocacy language
matters, because self-advocacy is precisely the skill the college transition demands.
(cid:127) On-campus occupational therapy and contracted speech-language services exist at the school level
(family-paid), indicating standing infrastructure for documented needs.

The School Profile as an instrument
The document colleges receive alongside every transcript is public, and it is the most aggressive piece of
institutional leverage Providence Day has. Rather than adjectives, it hands the admissions reader verifiable
numbers: complete per-subject AP score distributions under a mandatory-exam policy, SAT/ACT means and
mid-50% ranges, a GPA-quintile table with mean test scores per quintile, the weighting scale, and stated AP
course-load norms. The practical effect for an individual student: a reader can place a 3.9 weighted GPA or
four-AP senior schedule in precise context without trusting the school's word for it.
(cid:127) The Profile also carries the honor-code structure (student-run Honor Council), the 30-hour Senior Project
internship requirement, the Global Studies Diploma, and the no-rank policy — each a contextualizing
device for individual applicants.
(cid:127) Integrity signaling: the Profile states that Providence Day reports behavioral probations, suspensions, and
expulsions through graduation to colleges that request it, and that the school complies with NACAC's Guide
to Ethical Practice. Schools that disclose this earn reader trust that transfers to their advocacy for students.
Relationships with admissions offices
(cid:127) ~150 college representatives visit campus each year (school-published figure), concentrated in the fall
travel season — among the heaviest rep-visit traffic reported by any Charlotte-area school and a proxy for
how colleges prioritize the building.
(cid:127) The counseling team travels the other direction: 50+ campus visits per year, plus conference
presentations and service on regional and national admissions boards and organizations — relationship
maintenance in both directions.
(cid:127) A former university admissions officer sits inside the office (UC Berkeley undergraduate admissions),
bringing reader-side perspective to how student files are framed.
(cid:127) Institutional memberships: NAIS, NCAIS, SAIS, ERB, NACAC, SACAC, ACCIS, and the College Board.
Publication gap — named relationships and interviews
Providence Day does not publish claims of special relationships with specific elite admissions offices, and no
public material documents on-campus evaluative interviews with visiting reps. Schools essentially never
publish the former (doing so would be a NACAC ethics problem), so the absence is expected — a
transparency gap inherent to the industry, not a Providence Day weakness.
