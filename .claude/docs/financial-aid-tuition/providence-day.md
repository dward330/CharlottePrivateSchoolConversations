About this report
What it covers
The published price of attending Providence Day School: tuition at every price band the school publishes, the
mandatory and commonly-elected costs stacked on top of it, how the tuition-assistance process works, and
what the school discloses about how many families receive assistance and how large awards are. Where the
school has not published something, this report says so and names the question a family should put to the
business office.
Two words that are easy to confuse
Tuition assistance means need-based help with K–12 tuition at this school. That is what this report is about.
College financial aid — the FAFSA, the CSS Profile, and college merit scholarships — is a different subject
entirely and is not covered here. Providence Day publishes large college merit-scholarship totals for its
graduating classes; those are awards made by colleges to graduates, not assistance the school provides to
enrolled families, and they appear nowhere in the figures below.
Unpublished is not deficient
A number of figures in this report land as “the school does not publish this.” That is a statement about
disclosure and nothing more. It is never a finding that the school's assistance programme is small, restrictive, or
weak. Independent schools vary widely in how much of their aid data they put on the public record, and a
school that publishes less is not thereby doing less.
Published is not current
Every dollar figure and every percentage in this report carries the school year or filing year it applies to. Tuition
resets annually and retired pages linger in search results, so a figure without a year attached is not usable.
Several widely-circulated third-party listings for this school carry figures that do not match the school's own
current page; those are flagged in Section 7.
Describe, never appraise
This report reports the number, the year, and what the number includes. It draws no conclusion about what any
of it means for a household budget. That judgment belongs to the reader.
Aid data is reported in aggregate only
No family, student, or award recipient is named or described in any way that could identify them. Named
endowment funds appear by their institutional titles only — those identify a fund, never a recipient.

Reconciling the bands to the grade span
The school describes itself as an independent college-preparatory school for Transitional Kindergarten through
Grade 12. The four published bands account for all fourteen grade levels in that span: TK and Kindergarten are

and no overlap. A family looking for a figure specific to, say, Grade 3 will not find one — the Grade 1–5 rate is
the published figure for that grade.

Which platform, and what it costs to apply
Providence Day partners with Clarity to assess financial need. The application fee is $65, paid by the family, as
published for the 2026–27 cycle. The school describes the application as taking under an hour and as
mobile-friendly, and it runs family information webinars at which Clarity walks through the application, the tax
verification process, and how to get help.
What the family has to supply
(cid:127) A 1040 income tax return, W-2 forms, and the most recent pay stub
(cid:127) Tax documents transfer automatically from the I.R.S. once verification is complete
(cid:127) Business tax returns where a parent is self-employed, and 1099s
(cid:127) Possibly proof of expenses, and other financial information on request
(cid:127) Documents from both parents — custodial and non-custodial, whether divorced, separated or never
married. The school states that missing information from either parent may affect eligibility
How need is assessed
Clarity weighs gross income, assets, liabilities, family size, and the number of children enrolled in tuition-based
schools. After deducting basic living expenses and taxes, it evaluates the discretionary income remaining for
education. The school states that assistance does not exceed a family's demonstrated need, and that awards
depend both on demonstrated need and on the availability of school funds.

The one current figure, and what is missing from it
The school's live tuition and financial assistance page states: approximately 21% of Providence Day School
families receive some form of financial assistance. Two things about that sentence matter.
First, it carries no school year. The page was last published in July 2026 and states 2026–27 tuition rates, but
the percentage is not itself dated, so this report does not attach a year to it. Second, it names families as the
unit but does not state the base — whether the 21% is a share of all enrolled families, or a share of families who
applied for assistance. Those are materially different claims, and the source does not choose between them.
This report therefore reports the percentage as published, attaches no denominator, and derives no headcount
from it. Converting a rounded percentage into a count of real families would require choosing a base the school
has not specified.

An internal discrepancy, reported rather than resolved
The 2012–13 Report on Philanthropy states $3,105,191 as total financial aid awarded on its enrollment page,
and $3,092,165 as the financial aid line in its expense breakdown on the following page. The two figures differ
by $13,026. Both are published by the school in the same document. This report reproduces both and does not
choose between them; the likeliest explanation is that the figures are drawn on different accounting bases, but
the school does not say so, and inferring it would be a guess. It is a good question for the business office.
A unit change that blocks comparison
The Report on Philanthropy editions count students receiving financial aid. The current tuition page counts
families receiving some form of financial assistance. Those are different units measured against different
bases, and the apparent movement from 16% to 21% cannot be read as growth in aid reach — it may be
entirely an artefact of the change in unit, entirely real, or some mixture. This report declines to reconcile them
and flags the change so a reader is not misled by the juxtaposition.
How aid is funded
Assistance is drawn from both operating funds and endowment income. In 2017–18 the school reported
financial aid and discounts at 9.7% of expenses against a stated total budget of $40,600,000, and reported
that 5% of Annual Fund gifts that year were designated by donors to Student Financial Assistance. For 2012–13
the school published a financial aid expense line of $3,092,165 within a $33,320,795 expense total; that is an
expense share of 9.3% — our arithmetic on the school's own two figures, not a percentage the school states.
The endowment side is detailed in Section 5.

Two funds for the costs that sit outside tuition
The Murdock fund and the Latchwood endowment are unusual enough to note separately. Both exist to help
students with demonstrated need meet costs above and beyond tuition — the published descriptions name
tutoring, review classes, calculators, class trips, sports equipment, team camps, tournaments, dining hall
expenses and extracurricular activity fees. These are the same categories that appear as unpriced line items in
Section 2. Their existence confirms those costs are real and that the school has built dedicated mechanisms to
help with them.
Aid for children of faculty and staff
The Kristina C. Brockmeier Endowment for Financial Aid provides assistance annually to children of current
faculty and staff who demonstrate financial need. This is a need-based endowed award, not a blanket tuition
remission benefit. Whether the school separately offers a remission percentage to employees is not published.
Establishing a fund
The minimum gift required to establish a named endowment fund is $100,000, raised from $50,000 effective 1
July 2013. The school's donor-recognition materials list a Student Aid Fund naming level at $250,000.

The tuition trend could not be built
This section normally carries three to five years of published tuition rates and the year-over-year change. For
Providence Day it does not. The school's tuition page shows only the current year and is overwritten each cycle,
and archived snapshots of prior-year rate cards were not retrieved by the methods used here. Rather than
substitute third-party figures for the school's own rate cards, this report reports no tuition trend. That is a
limitation of this report, not a disclosure failure by the school — publishing only the current year is ordinary
practice.
A navigation hazard worth naming
Widely-circulated third-party listings for Providence Day carry tuition figures that do not agree with each other or
with the school's own page. Figures in circulation include a single “approximately $20,500” rate, a “$34,430”

The aid trend the school does publish
Total financial aid awarded rose from $3,105,191 in 2012–13 to $3,683,971 in 2017–18, with a dip to
$3,013,928 in 2013–14 in between. The average grant rose across the same window from $12,273 to $13,695.
The share of students receiving aid was 16% at both ends of that window. Enrollment grew from 1,552 to 1,627
students, and the number of families from 1,046 to 1,073, over the same period. No comparable figures were
located for any year after 2017–18, so whether these trends continued is unknown.
Institutional scale, from the I.R.S. filings
Total revenue reported to the I.R.S. grew from $44,654,804 in the fiscal year ending June 2019 to $72,890,636
in the fiscal year ending June 2025. Endowment market value, as published by the school, grew from
$6,486,511.97 in June 2013 to $14,029,706.59 as of 30 June 2018. The school's published strategic framework
states a goal of increasing the endowment to $55 million by 2030. Because the aid budget is funded partly
from endowment income, endowment growth is relevant context — though the school does not publish what
share of endowment income is directed to assistance.

The middle-income question
Families whose income sits above the assistance threshold but below comfortable full-pay are the group least
addressed by published materials at most independent schools, and Providence Day is no exception. The
school publishes no guidance aimed at that group, no income bands, and no illustrative examples of what a
family at a given income might expect. This is a publication gap. It is worth noting that the school does publish
the existence of the Murdock and Latchwood funds for costs above tuition, which is a partial answer to one part
of the question, and that Clarity's methodology explicitly accounts for the number of children a family has in
tuition-charging schools.
